GDPR and AI (Personal Data in Training and Use)

regulationfreshness: volatilesources not yet verified

The EU General Data Protection Regulation applies whenever AI systems process personal data — in training sets, prompts, logs and outputs. This entry orients developers; it is not legal advice.

Short answer: GDPR covers personal data wherever it appears in an AI workflow: you need a lawful basis, data minimisation, transparency, security, a DPIA for high-risk processing, and a way to honour data-subject rights such as access and erasure. How erasure applies to trained models is still being worked out by regulators; take legal advice.

What is it?

If an AI system touches information about identifiable people — names, emails, support chats, photos, behaviour — European data-protection law applies, even when the data is only a small part of a huge training set or just passes through a prompt. It asks you to have a good reason for using the data, use no more than you need, keep it safe, be open about it, and let people exercise their rights.

Also known as: GDPR, GDPR and AI, right to erasure, right to be forgotten, data subject rights, DPIA, data protection impact assessment, lawful basis.

Technical definition

GDPR is Regulation (EU) 2016/679. Core principles (Art. 5): lawfulness, fairness and transparency; purpose limitation; data minimisation; accuracy; storage limitation; integrity and confidentiality; accountability. Controllers need a lawful basis (Art. 6; special categories need more, Art. 9). Data-subject rights include access, rectification, erasure, restriction, portability and objection (Arts. 15–21); Art. 22 limits solely automated decisions with significant effects. Controller–processor contracts, records of processing, data-protection by design and by default, Data Protection Impact Assessments (Art. 35) and restrictions on international transfers also apply. For AI, open questions include whether and when a trained model itself contains personal data, how erasure and objection can be honoured after training (machine unlearning is an active research area), and legitimate-interest reliance for training; the European Data Protection Board has issued opinions on AI models (verify the current opinions and national authority guidance). GDPR operates alongside the EU AI Act (EU AI Act).

Entity type: regulation · Freshness class: volatile.

When should I use it?

Relevant whenever AI work involves people in the EU/EEA or an organisation subject to GDPR, which in practice covers most customer-facing AI products. This is orientation, not legal advice.

Implementation patterns

  • Map where personal data enters prompts, retrieval indexes, logs, fine-tuning sets and outputs.
  • Minimise and redact before sending data to a model or vendor; prefer vendor terms that exclude training on your data and set retention.
  • Run a DPIA early for high-risk or large-scale processing, and revisit it when the system changes.
  • Keep a way to find and delete a person's data in indexes, logs and datasets you control.
  • Provide meaningful information and human review where automated decisions significantly affect people.
  • Involve your data-protection officer or counsel in design.

Technologies and frameworks

Commonly associated: data mapping and records of processing, DPIA templates, PII detection and redaction, data processing agreements, privacy-preserving techniques (anonymisation, pseudonymisation, differential privacy). Names are examples of what exists, not recommendations, and projects change quickly.

Practical guide

  1. Inventory the personal data in your AI workflow.
  2. Decide the lawful basis and purposes, and update privacy notices.
  3. Minimise, redact and pseudonymise where possible.
  4. Check vendor contracts, sub-processors and transfer mechanisms.
  5. Run a DPIA for high-risk uses and document decisions.
  6. Build deletion and access processes that reach every store, including indexes and logs.
  7. Review with legal or privacy specialists.

Common failure modes

  • Treating prompts and logs as "not really personal data".
  • No lawful basis or transparency notice for using customer data in training.
  • Sending personal data to a vendor without a processing agreement or transfer mechanism.
  • Indexing personal data into a vector store with no deletion route.
  • Assuming anonymisation is complete when re-identification is possible.
  • Relying on a model vendor's marketing as a compliance guarantee.

Troubleshooting

  • A user asks you to delete their data — Locate and delete it in source stores, vector indexes, caches and logs; record what you did; seek advice on any data already used to train a model.
  • Unsure whether a DPIA is needed — Assess scale, sensitivity, profiling and automated decision-making; when in doubt, do one.
  • Vendor terms unclear on training or retention — Get written terms or use a configuration/plan that disables training and sets retention; or process locally.

Contrasted with

  • EU AI Act — The AI Act regulates AI systems by risk category; GDPR regulates processing of personal data. Both can apply to the same system.
